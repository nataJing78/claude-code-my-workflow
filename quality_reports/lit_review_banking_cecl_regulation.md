## Summary

The Current Expected Credit Loss (CECL) standard (ASU 2016-13, ASC 326) replaced the incurred-loss (IL) model for U.S. GAAP filers: large SEC filers adopted on 1 January 2020, and all remaining banks on 1 January 2023. Because the effective date coincided with the COVID-19 shock, and because the CARES Act made 2020 adoption optional for a subset of banks, the last five years produced an unusually clean set of natural experiments. The literature has moved quickly from pre-adoption simulation and theory (2018–2020) to post-adoption empirical work, and by 2024–2026 the core findings are appearing in the top accounting journals (*The Accounting Review*, *Journal of Accounting Research*, *Journal of Accounting and Economics*, *Contemporary Accounting Research*).

Three findings are now well established. **First, CECL made provisions more forward-looking and timelier.** Kim, Kim, Kleymenova & Li (TAR 2026) show CECL banks' provisions better reflect future local economic conditions and that CECL banks experience fewer subsequent defaults; the Federal Reserve's own pandemic post-mortem (Loudis et al. 2021) documents that adopters' allowances rose sharply and early in 2020. **Second, CECL has real effects on credit supply, concentrated in capital-constrained banks and in stress periods.** Chen, Dou, Ryan & Zou (TAR 2025) find adopters cut loan growth more during the COVID recession, especially low-capital banks; Yang (TAR 2025) finds capital-constrained banks slowed total and residential loan growth after CECL's approval; Granja & Nagel (JAE 2025) find modest consumer-loan rate increases, larger for weakly capitalized banks and disadvantaged borrowers; Hu (SSRN 2024) finds reduced small-business originations in distant markets. **Third, the procyclicality question remains genuinely contested.** The theory (Mahieux, Sapra & Zhang, JAR 2023) says timely provisioning enables earlier intervention but changes ex ante loan origination in either direction, and that capital regulation should be looser under CECL only when early loss estimates are precise enough or risk-shifting incentives are mild; the COVID evidence is split between "CECL amplified the credit contraction" (Chen et al. 2025; Chen & Huang, JAR 2026 for China's ECL adopters) and "CECL reduced lending's sensitivity to the cycle" (Chen, Lin & Yang, SSRN 2023, on mortgage approvals and house prices; Loudis et al. 2021 finding no clear lending effect).

A second strand concerns **information and discretion**. Evidence on whether CECL improved the information environment is mixed: Gee, Neilson, Schmidt & Xie (SSRN 2022, revised) find CECL allowances are decision-useful for both small and large banks, and Wheeler (JAR 2021) shows investors priced unrecognized expected losses even before adoption; but Bonsall, Schmidt & Xie (SSRN 2025) and Bonaldi, Liang & Yang (SSRN 2023) find analyst forecast properties and earnings-announcement informativeness deteriorated, and Wheeler (SSRN 2025) finds CECL raised the macroeconomic content of provisions while lowering bank-specific credit-loss content. Discretion has not disappeared: European IFRS 9 evidence (Behn & Couaillier, JAE 2026; Novotny-Farkas, Oberson & Renner, SSRN 2024) shows capital-constrained banks provision less for the same exposures, and U.S. community-bank evidence (He, Mamun & Mishra, BAR 2025) shows income smoothing persists. A newer sub-strand studies forecast quality itself: Bhojraj, Kleymenova, Liu, Lu & Sengupta (JAE forthcoming) document overreaction in banks' supervisory loss forecasts, and Canals-Cerdá (Philadelphia Fed 2024, 2026) shows CECL is mechanically more sensitive to macro-forecast error than IL.

The most active frontiers as of 2026 are (i) distributional effects of expected-loss provisioning on household and SME credit access, (ii) the interaction between CECL allowances and the supervisory stress test / stress capital buffer, which the Federal Reserve has explicitly deferred, and (iii) the 2023 adoption wave at community banks, which offers a second, non-pandemic natural experiment that is still largely unexploited.

## Key Papers

Ordered roughly by relevance to "CECL regulation and banks." Publication status noted (published / forthcoming / working paper).

### Mahieux, Sapra & Zhang (2023) — CECL: Timely Loan Loss Provisioning and Bank Regulation
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting Research* 61(1): 3–46.
- **Main contribution:** The canonical theory paper. Models the trade-off between accuracy and timeliness in loss recognition and asks how CECL/IFRS 9 should interact with capital regulation.
- **Method:** Analytical model of a bank with asset-substitution incentives and a regulator who can intervene.
- **Key finding:** Timely provisioning improves efficiency by allowing earlier intervention against ex post asset substitution, but banks anticipate that intervention and originate *either safer or riskier* loans ex ante depending on the regime. Regulatory capital under CECL should be looser than under incurred loss only when early loss estimates are sufficiently precise and/or risk-shifting incentives are not too severe; the result is conditional, not a blanket call for lower capital.
- **Relevance:** Provides the benchmark predictions (capital-constrained banks react most; ex ante origination-incentive channel) that the empirical papers below test.

### Kim, Kim, Kleymenova & Li (2026) — CECL Standard and Banks' Information Production
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 101(3): 493–526. Earlier versions: FEDS Working Paper 2023-063; SSRN 4117869 / 4601190.
- **Main contribution:** First systematic evidence that CECL changed what banks *know*, not only what they report.
- **Method:** Difference-in-differences comparing 2020 adopters to non-adopters (CARES Act deferral), with local-economic-conditions data and textual analysis of provision disclosures.
- **Key finding:** CECL banks' provisions become timelier and better reflect future local economic conditions; disclosures become longer, more forward-looking and more quantitative; CECL banks experience lower loan default rates. Effects are stronger for banks investing more in systems and human capital.
- **Relevance:** The strongest "benefit" result in the literature; the counterweight to the real-effects cost literature.

### Chen, Dou, Ryan & Zou (2025) — CECL and Banks' Lending during Stress Periods: Evidence from the COVID-19 Recession
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 100(1): 113–138. SSRN 4110109 (earlier title: "Does the Current Expected Credit Loss Approach Decrease the Procyclicality of Banks' Lending?").
- **Main contribution:** Direct test of the procyclicality concern using the 2020 adoption / CARES deferral split.
- **Method:** DiD on quarterly Call Report data; heterogeneity by regulatory capital and loan impairment; county-level real outcomes.
- **Key finding:** Adopters increased provisions and reduced loan growth more than non-adopters during the recession. The contraction is stronger for adopters with low regulatory capital and low impairment, and concentrated in commercial loans. Counties with higher CECL-adopter market share saw larger unemployment increases and slower recoveries.
- **Relevance:** The leading "CECL is procyclical in a downturn" result; anchors the open debate.

### Yang (2025) — How Does Loan Loss Accounting Influence Bank Lending? Evidence from CECL
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 100(1): 465–490 (DOI 10.2308/TAR-2022-0511).
- **Main contribution:** Isolates the *capital-cost* channel of CECL from the pandemic by using the standard's approval (2016), not its effective date.
- **Method:** DiD around CECL's approval, exploiting cross-sectional variation in capital constraints.
- **Key finding:** Capital-constrained banks reduce growth of total loans and residential loans after approval. One search-result abstract also reports that they sell more of the residential loans they originate shortly after origination (originate-to-distribute); this secondary result was not independently corroborated in verification.
- **Relevance:** Shows lending effects pre-date and are independent of COVID; the securitization margin is a novel adjustment channel.

### Granja & Nagel (2025) — Current Expected Credit Losses and Consumer Loans
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 80(1): 101781. SSRN 4324118.
- **Main contribution:** Loan-level evidence on pricing and quantity effects of CECL in consumer credit.
- **Method:** TransUnion credit-bureau data covering millions of consumer loans; adopter vs. non-adopter design with borrower-level heterogeneity.
- **Key finding:** Higher reserve requirements after CECL induce a statistically significant but economically moderate increase in loan interest rates; effects are larger for weakly capitalized banks and larger still for disadvantaged borrowers at weakly capitalized banks.
- **Relevance:** Best-identified pricing evidence; introduces the distributional dimension.

### Aaron, Jia, Ng & Zhang (2025) — Current Expected Credit Loss Model Adoption
- **Status:** Published, *Contemporary Accounting Research* 42(4): 2915–2948 (DOI 10.1111/1911-3846.13078).
- **Main contribution:** Treats the 2020 adoption decision itself as the outcome, addressing the selection problem in the DiD literature above.
- **Method:** Cross-section of the adoption choice under the CARES Act option, then panel analysis of pandemic-era provisioning.
- **Key finding:** Banks more exposed to the pandemic were *less* likely to adopt CECL in 2020, more so when they had more lending opportunities, more competition, or worse loan quality. Adopters reported higher provisions early in the pandemic and lower or negative provisions later.
- **Relevance:** Essential caveat for every 2020-adopter DiD: adoption was endogenous to pandemic exposure.

### Ertan (2025) — Expected Losses, Unexpected Costs? Evidence from SME Credit Access under IFRS 9
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 100(4): 443–473. SSRN 3504708.
- **Main contribution:** The cleanest evidence on expected-loss provisioning and SME credit, using Europe's IFRS 9 transition.
- **Method:** Bank-, borrower- and loan-contract-level DiD around IFRS 9 adoption.
- **Key finding:** Affected banks cut SME lending by more than 10%; rejection rates, spreads and collateral requirements rose; loan amounts, maturities and borrower investment fell. Effects persisted into COVID.
- **Relevance:** Complements Hu (2024) on U.S. small-business lending; suggests opaque, bank-dependent borrowers bear the cost of forward-looking provisioning.

### Chen & Huang (2026) — A Tale of Two Banks: When Credit Loss Models Meet Economic Crises
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting Research* (DOI 10.1111/1475-679X.70030). SSRN 5719602.
- **Main contribution:** Administrative loan-level and firm-level evidence from China, where ECL and ICL banks coexisted at the onset of COVID.
- **Key finding:** ECL banks reduced credit supply and became more prudent after the pandemic began relative to ICL banks.
- **Relevance:** External validity for the Chen et al. (2025) U.S. result; shows the procyclicality pattern is not a U.S. artifact.

### Behn & Couaillier (2026) — Weak Capital, Weak Provisions: Credit Risk Provisioning under IFRS 9
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 81(3). ECB Working Paper 2841 ("Same same but different"); SSRN 4545496.
- **Method:** Euro-area credit-register (AnaCredit-type) data allowing within-borrower, within-period comparison across banks.
- **Key finding:** Less-capitalized banks provision less than better-capitalized peers *for the same loan to the same borrower in the same period*. Most provisioning still occurs at default; aggregate provisioning patterns are not fundamentally changed by IFRS 9.
- **Relevance:** Strongest evidence that expected-loss discretion is used for capital management; directly relevant to the U.S. debate on CECL model overlays.

### Qiang & Wang (2024) — CECL and Conditional Conservatism of Banks, with Spillover to Borrowers
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 99(6): 389–420. SSRN 4868246.
- **Key finding:** Under CECL banks increase conservatism in loan losses but decrease conservatism in other earnings components; their borrowers increase conditional conservatism by about 40%, roughly twice the banks' own increase.
- **Relevance:** Introduces the borrower-side reporting spillover; supports the "banks monitor harder under ECL" mechanism also found by Li, Ng & Saffar (Management Science 2024) for IFRS 9.

### Lu & Nikolaev (2022) — Expected Loan Loss Provisioning: An Empirical Model
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 97(7): 319–346. SSRN 3344657.
- **Main contribution:** A benchmark model for lifetime expected losses.
- **Method:** Cross-sectional loss prediction combined with a high-dimensional dynamic factor model of aggregate losses over the cycle; out-of-sample evaluation.
- **Key finding:** Predicts long-horizon losses substantially more accurately than Harris, Khan & Nissim (2018) and other alternatives, and detects bank failures better.
- **Relevance:** Useful as an external benchmark for "how much of reported CECL allowance is predictable"; feeds the Wheeler (2025) informativeness question.

### Bhojraj, Kleymenova, Liu, Lu & Sengupta (2025) — Imperfect Expectations in Loan Loss Forecasts
- **Status:** Accepted, *Journal of Accounting and Economics* (per authors' site); SSRN 4858012 (June 2024); presented at Boston Fed stress-testing conference 2025.
- **Method:** Confidential FR Y-14A supervisory forecasts of net charge-offs; tests of forecast-error predictability.
- **Key finding:** Revisions of charge-off forecasts are negatively associated with subsequent forecast errors (overreaction, consistent with the representativeness heuristic), stronger at longer horizons and for banks with longer-maturity loans; overreaction also appears in banks' regional macro forecasts.
- **Relevance:** If banks overreact, CECL (which loads on long-horizon forecasts) will be *more* procyclical than rational-expectations models predict.

### Beatty & Liao (2021) — What Do Analysts' Provision Forecasts Tell Us about Expected Credit Loss Recognition?
- **Status:** Published, *The Accounting Review* 96(1): 1–21.
- **Key finding:** Controlling for recognized provisions, consensus analyst provision forecasts are incrementally associated with future NPLs and returns, implying the IL regime left decision-useful expected-loss information unrecognized.
- **Relevance:** Pre-adoption anchor for the "CECL adds information" hypothesis.

### Wheeler (2021) — Unrecognized Expected Credit Losses and Bank Share Prices
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting Research* 59(3): 805–866.
- **Key finding:** A vintage-analysis measure of lifetime expected losses is negatively priced by the market, more strongly for large banks; recorded allowances were on average below estimated expected losses, foreshadowing capital effects on adoption.
- **Relevance:** Together with Beatty & Liao, establishes what investors could infer pre-CECL. Wheeler's 2025 SSRN follow-up ("Assessing CECL") finds CECL raised macro content but lowered bank-specific content of provisions.

### López-Espinosa, Ormazabal & Sakasai (2021) — Switching from Incurred to Expected Loan Loss Provisioning: Early Evidence
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting Research* 59(3): 757–804.
- **Relevance:** The first large-sample IFRS 9 transition paper; the European anchor for the ECL-vs-ICL comparison.

### Kim (2022) — Delays in Banks' Loan Loss Provisioning and Economic Downturns: Evidence from the U.S. Housing Market
- **Status:** Published, *Journal of Accounting Research* 60(3): 711–754.
- **Relevance:** Quantifies the cost of the IL regime's delayed recognition in the 2007–09 housing bust, motivating CECL.

### Loudis, Pechenik, Ranish, Vojtech & Xu (2021) — New Accounting Framework Faces Its First Test: CECL During the Pandemic
- **Status:** FEDS Notes, December 2021.
- **Key finding:** Day-one adoption raised allowances ~37%; excluding adoption effects, allowances rose ~76% in H1 2020 vs. 2019Q4. Adopters' provisioning was more responsive to the outlook, with wide variation by loan type; limited evidence that CECL reduced lending in the pandemic. Consumer loan growth was ~3 pp lower at CECL banks in 2020, driven by credit cards and other consumer loans, with no effect on C&I, CRE, or residential real estate.
- **Relevance:** The regulator's own read; note the contrast with Chen et al. (2025) on commercial lending.

### Hu (2024) — The Real Effects of CECL on Banks' Small Business Lending
- **Status:** Working paper, SSRN 4934238 (August 2024).
- **Key finding:** Adopters cut small-business originations in counties far from their branches, concentrated in loans under $100k; no such pattern for purchased loans or lien mortgages. Interpreted as CECL raising information-production costs for opaque distant borrowers.

### Canals-Cerdá (2024, 2026) — CECL Implementation and Model Risk (Philadelphia Fed WP 24-03; superseded by WP 26-09)
- **Status:** Working papers, Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia.
- **Key finding:** CECL is mechanically more sensitive to macro-forecast and model-specification error than IL; a flexible machine-learning modelling framework can be estimated quickly on tens of millions of consumer loans without forecasting-performance penalty.
- **Relevance:** The model-risk literature; relevant to the "reasonable and supportable forecast" debate.

### Novotny-Farkas, Oberson & Renner (2024) — IFRS 9 under Stress
- **Status:** Working paper, SSRN 4918921 (August 2024).
- **Key finding:** European quarterly data 2018–2022 show income smoothing, delayed recognition and subjective post-model adjustments; provisions on unimpaired assets are procyclical but prudential filters mitigated lending procyclicality in COVID; under-provisioning on impaired assets is concentrated in weakly capitalized and COVID-exposed banks and associated with reduced lending.

## Thematic Organization

### Theoretical Contributions

- **Timeliness vs. accuracy and the capital-regulation interaction** (Mahieux, Sapra & Zhang 2023). Timely recognition is efficient when it enables intervention, but it changes ex ante origination incentives in a regime-dependent direction; capital requirements should fall with timelier provisioning only when early loss estimates are precise or risk-shifting incentives are mild. Related earlier models (Abad & Suarez 2018; Chae et al. 2018) are pre-window but remain the reference for procyclicality simulations.
- **Forecast imperfection** (Vidinova 2023, SSRN 4628656, "Forward-Looking Loan Loss Provisioning under Imperfect Forecasts"; Bhojraj et al. 2025). If forecasts are noisy or biased, forward-looking provisioning can be *more* procyclical than incurred-loss provisioning.
- **Structural quantification** (Huber 2022, SSRN 4120628). A structural bank model calibrated to U.S. data reports that CECL raises reserves and reduces lending; two circulating versions give different magnitudes (14.7% reserves / 5.16% lending in one, 16% / 3.15% in another), so cite the dated SSRN version explicitly.
- **Evergreening** (Eufinger, Ramirez-Chiang & Sakasai 2026, SSRN 7216299). ECL weakens banks' incentive to roll over loans to non-viable "zombie" firms because forward-looking provisions must be booked regardless; empirically, earlier IFRS 9 implementers cut lending growth to zombies.

### Empirical Findings

**(a) Provisioning behaviour and timeliness.** Consensus that CECL/IFRS 9 provisions respond faster to the outlook (Loudis et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2026; Elashmawy & Kallunki 2026 for Europe, who also find IFRS 9 provisions predict fewer NPL adverse events and lower charge-offs). Volatility rose: Jia, Kanagaretnam & Zhang (JAAF 2025) report higher provision and allowance volatility for adopters, alongside timelier and more valid provisions and higher earnings response coefficients (the specific magnitudes quoted in some summaries, about half a standard deviation and a doubling of volatility, were not verifiable here); Bonaldi, Liang & Yang (2023) show adopters were more likely to book negative provisions in late 2020.

**(b) Discretion did not go away.** Behn & Couaillier (2026) and Novotny-Farkas et al. (2024) in Europe; He, Mamun & Mishra (BAR 2025) show U.S. community banks smooth income via provisions and shifted toward expected-loss behaviour before formal 2023 adoption; the Kansas City Fed (2023) documents a modest average day-one allowance increase of 3.76% at community banks in 2023, larger for $1–10bn banks (7.53%) than for sub-$1bn banks (3.08%), attributed to smaller banks' heavier pre-CECL use of qualitative factors.

**(c) Real effects on credit.** Contraction in stress (Chen et al. 2025; Chen & Huang 2026), capital-cost channel independent of stress (Yang 2025), pricing (Granja & Nagel 2025), SME/small-business access (Ertan 2025; Hu 2024), mortgage allocation (Research Square 2026 preprint linking post-CECL provision pressure to larger Black–White denial gaps in home-improvement and refinance channels at banks over $10bn; author not identified in search results, treat as preliminary). Counter-evidence: Chen, Lin & Yang (2023) find mortgage approval rates became *less* sensitive to house-price growth after CECL (their secondary claim of dampened local housing booms was not independently corroborated); Loudis et al. (2021) find no C&I/CRE/RRE effect. Asymmetry: Buchetti, Perdichizzi & Santoni (Economics Letters 2025) show lending contracts immediately on IFRS 9 stage downgrades but recovers only slowly on upgrades.

**(d) Information environment and capital markets.** Positive: Gee et al. (2022, revised) — CECL allowances decision-useful for small and large banks, new information mainly for small banks; Kim et al. (2026) — richer disclosure; JAPP 2025 ("Expected loss recognition and banks' management forecasts") — banks issue more, and more precise, management forecasts after ELM adoption worldwide; Hu (2024b) — negative information spillovers from bellwether banks' provisions to non-announcing banks and constrained non-financial firms shrink after CECL. Negative: Bonsall, Schmidt & Xie (2022, 2025) — analyst provision forecasts less accurate, more dispersed, lower coverage; earnings-announcement informativeness falls most where CECL raises provision volatility and where sophisticated investors are present; Bable, Wong & Wynes (2023) — interviewed users perceive lower decision usefulness and comparability; Wheeler (2025) — more macro, less bank-specific information.

**(e) Spillovers to borrowers.** Qiang & Wang (2024) on borrower conservatism; Li, Ng & Saffar (Management Science 2024) show IFRS 9-driven monitoring shifts firms from bank to public debt; Guo, Jia, Ng & Zhu (SSRN 2025) on cross-border borrowing.

### Methodological Innovations

- **CARES Act deferral as an instrument for adoption timing** (Chen et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2026; Bonaldi et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2025), with Aaron et al. (2025) showing the choice is endogenous to pandemic exposure — later papers should model selection or use approval-date designs (Yang 2025).
- **Credit-register within-borrower designs** (Behn & Couaillier 2026; Chen & Huang 2026; Ertan 2025) that hold borrower demand fixed.
- **Credit-bureau loan-level pricing data** (Granja & Nagel 2025) and **HMDA application-level data** (Chen, Lin & Yang 2023; Research Square 2026).
- **Supervisory forecast data (FR Y-14A)** for testing forecast rationality (Bhojraj et al. 2025).
- **Textual measures of forward-looking disclosure** (Kim et al. 2026).
- **Out-of-sample lifetime-loss benchmarks** (Lu & Nikolaev 2022) and ML model-risk frameworks (Canals-Cerdá 2024/2026).

### Open Debates

1. **Is CECL procyclical?** Theory says "depends on capital regulation"; U.S. COVID evidence says yes for commercial lending at low-capital banks (Chen et al. 2025) but no for mortgage approvals (Chen, Lin & Yang 2023) and no aggregate effect per the Fed (Loudis et al. 2021). COVID's fiscal support and regulatory relief (capital transition, PPP) confound all of these.
2. **Did information quality rise or fall?** Bank-level information production improved (Kim et al.), but external users (analysts, interviewees) report worse predictability; Wheeler (2025) reconciles part of this as a macro-vs-idiosyncratic composition shift.
3. **How much discretion remains, and is it harmful?** European evidence says capital management survives ECL; U.S. evidence on post-model overlays ("Q-factors") under CECL is thin.
4. **Should CECL enter the supervisory stress test?** The Federal Reserve's 2024–2025 stress-test proposals explicitly do *not* incorporate CECL, citing that the current allowance framework already projects four quarters ahead; industry analysis (BPI) argues incorporation would raise capital requirements via larger peak-to-trough declines.

## Gaps and Opportunities

1. **The 2023 adoption wave is under-studied.** Roughly all remaining U.S. banks adopted on 1 Jan 2023 in a non-crisis, rising-rate environment. Only descriptive work (KC Fed 2023; He et al. 2025) exists. A DiD around 2023 with 2020 adopters as controls would separate CECL's effect from COVID and from the CARES selection problem.
2. **Distributional effects.** Granja & Nagel (2025), Ertan (2025) and the 2026 mortgage preprint suggest costs fall on disadvantaged or opaque borrowers. Fair-lending implications of provision-driven allocation are essentially untested with rigorous identification.
3. **Model overlays and "reasonable and supportable" forecasts.** No published U.S. paper measures the size, timing or capital-sensitivity of qualitative adjustments under CECL, although disclosure text (Kim et al.) and supervisory data (Bhojraj et al.) make it feasible.
4. **CECL × capital regulation.** Mahieux et al. predict optimal capital should fall when early loss estimates are precise; the five-year transition ended for 2020 adopters in 2025. Whether the end of transition relief changed lending is an open, well-identified question.
5. **Reconciling procyclicality results.** Papers use different lending margins (loan growth vs. approval sensitivity vs. pricing) and horizons. A unified loan-level study across margins, or a structural model estimated on the post-2020 panel (extending Huber 2022), would help.
6. **Securities and non-loan assets.** Almost all work is about loans; CECL also applies to HTM securities and off-balance-sheet commitments (relevant after the 2023 regional-bank stress).
7. **Nonbank and credit-union adoption.** Credit unions adopted in 2023 with the NCUA simplified tool; no academic evidence yet.

- Method: for any new 2020-adopter DiD, model adoption selection (Aaron et al.) or use approval-date designs (Yang).
